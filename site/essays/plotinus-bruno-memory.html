Plotinus and Bruno: Memory as an Active Power of Soul

How Clucas's Neoplatonic reading reframes the mnemonic wheel as an instrument of ascent

Plotinus rejects the wax-tablet model of memory (perception as passive impression); memory is instead an active power of soul, strengthened by attention and exercise. Clucas argues this is the deep foundation of Bruno's claim that the 'higher art' orders all the operations of the soul, including appetite as well as intellect. The essay traces how Bruno's 'shadows' (umbrae) work as a two-directional structure -- descending from unity into multiplicity in the cosmos, and ascending from images back toward unity in the trained practitioner -- and argues the mnemonic wheel is best understood not as a database or a magical computer but as a model of the soul's movement through a structured universe.

Plotinus's fundamental break with the wax-tablet model is the starting point. In *Ennead* IV.6, 'Perception and Memory,' Plotinus attacks the model in which perception leaves an impression in the soul like a seal in wax, and memory is the retention of that impression. For Plotinus, perception is not passive reception and memory is not passive storage: the soul is a power that acts. Memory follows attention; repeated attention strengthens the soul's capacity; forgetting is the dispersal of attention, remembering is the soul's power recollected and brought back into operation.

This is exactly where Clucas's reading of Bruno becomes important. Bruno says his higher and more general art concerns ordering all the operations of the soul, including intellectual operations and appetition. Clucas therefore reads the mnemonic art as simultaneously a logic, an ethical discipline, and a form of magic -- not three separate projects layered onto one text, but one project described from three angles.

Plotinus's soul occupies an intermediate position between the intelligible and sensible worlds, and his account of memory changes depending on which direction the soul is operating. Turned toward intelligible realities, memory is a kind of reawakening -- the soul has a natural affinity with the intelligible and 'remembers' it as something already belonging to its own higher nature. Turned toward the sensible world, the soul actively brings things into renewed presence through its own power. Two quite different models: remembering something external, versus awakening something already implicit within the soul.

This distinction makes Bruno's 'shadows' (umbrae) far more dynamic than a simple copy-of-reality metaphor. For Bruno, per Clucas, the universe proceeds from unity into multiplicity through an ordered series of forms and similitudes -- the Thirty Intentions describe this as a descent from the One's light into increasing multiplicity. The mind encounters those multiplicities as images, concepts, species, signs. The mnemonic operation then works in the opposite direction: images -> relations -> forms -> unity. The image is potentially a vehicle of return, not merely a container for facts.

This also explains why Bruno's images are so strange -- grotesque, erotic, violent, mythological, comic, emotionally charged. If the goal were simple memorization, plain pictures would do. But Bruno wants images that carry relations: a single image encoding a word, a syllable, a myth, a natural object, an emotional association, a philosophical category, a planetary correspondence, a moral quality, a cosmological position, all at once. The practitioner learns to move through those relationships. Clucas's terms for this are simulacra and signacula: not labels attached to information, but participants in an ordered system of relations through which intellect and imagination operate.

Plotinus also explains Bruno's obsession with attention. Memory depends on attention -- scattered mental activity retains less, concentrated attention retains more, and repeated exercise strengthens the relevant power of soul. Bruno makes this architectural: instead of telling the practitioner 'pay attention,' he builds an entire world of attention-commanding images. The grotesque and erotic figures are not arbitrary ornament; they are designed to seize the imagination, so that once captured, attention can be connected to a larger conceptual system. Striking image -> attention -> memory -> association -> conceptual movement -- recognizably Plotinian psychology, put to much more elaborate use.

Why does Bruno need both intellect and imagination? Because purely abstract intellect is not enough for embodied human beings. Intellect grasps relations and principles; imagination supplies the images through which those relations become psychologically operative. The chain runs: intellect identifies a universal relation -> imagination embodies it in a memorable image -> attention fixes the image -> memory makes the relation available again -> intellect recognizes further relationships -> imagination reconfigures them -> the soul becomes increasingly ordered. That last step -- the soul becoming ordered -- is the specifically Brunian ambition, beyond anything Plotinus states explicitly.

Bruno should not be read as simply copying Plotinus. A more complicated picture -- Bruno combining Plotinian and Aristotelian elements such that a single psychological claim often cannot be traced to one source alone -- captures something Clucas's Plotinus-centered reading doesn't fully resolve. Aristotle supplies the necessity of images/phantasms for human cognition; Plotinus supplies the soul as active power and the unity-to-multiplicity metaphysical structure; Bruno's ars memoriae is the synthesis: construct images that let the mind manipulate multiplicities and retrace their relationships.

This reframes the magic. A magical image need not be imagined as a supernatural object broadcasting celestial energy. It can be magical because it changes the configuration of the soul -- what one attends to, remembers, desires, recognizes, does. Because Bruno holds a strong doctrine of the interconnectedness of things, transforming the soul is not necessarily isolated from the natural world. Clucas's threefold formulation -- memory art = logic + ethics + magic -- becomes powerful here: the logical dimension orders representations, the ethical dimension orders appetites and dispositions, the magical dimension concerns the efficacy of images and the bonds they establish between things. Complementary aspects of one project of human perfection and transformation.

The deepest connection, and the one most useful for interactive design, is memory as ascent. An ordinary mnemonic sees a horse and remembers 'horse' -- the image as hook. A Brunian mnemonic sees a horse embedded in a system: horse -> motion -> animal soul -> terrestrial life -> celestial influence -> multiplicity -> unity. The image becomes a node in a metaphysical network, and the practitioner learns not merely to remember the horse but to move through the network of relations it represents. The memory palace becomes an ascent machine -- fundamentally Plotinian in structure (movement from multiplicity toward intelligible unity, ultimately toward the One), but made operational and combinatorial by Bruno. The wheel performs, in miniature, both directions: many -> relation -> pattern -> form -> unity, and (because Bruno's universe is dynamic) unity -> differentiation -> images -> world. A miniature cosmology, not a database and not a magical computer, but a model of the soul's movement through a structured universe -- combining syllables and images at the surface, concepts at the intellectual level, relationships among forms at the philosophical level, attention at the psychological level, desire at the ethical level, and potentially the mind's return to its own source at the mystical level.

This is also why Clucas insists Bruno's memory treatises can't be separated from *Spaccio* and *Eroici furori*: the Italian dialogues dramatize, as narrative, the same transformation of the human subject that the technical mnemonic works try to make into a practical method. Actaeon does not merely illustrate contemplative transformation -- he undergoes, as story, exactly what a sufficiently potent image is theorized to do to whoever truly beholds it.
